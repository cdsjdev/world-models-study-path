Video Representations

A video model needs a compact representation that preserves appearance, motion, and temporal relationships for understanding, prediction, and generation. This topic explains how tokenization, attention, and latent spaces turn raw video into that model-ready representation.

Why video is not just images with a time axis

A single image is already a large object for a neural network: a 224×224 image has 50,176 pixels. A ten-second video at 24 frames per second and the same resolution has 240 frames: roughly 12 million pixels. Naively treating video as a stack of independent images throws away the one thing that makes video valuable: motion and change carry information that no single frame contains. A model that only sees frames in isolation cannot tell a cup falling from a cup being lifted.

Every idea in this section is an answer to the same underlying constraint: video is too large to process at the pixel level with full attention, and too structured (temporally redundant, spatially correlated) to treat as an unordered pile of tokens. The techniques below are the compromises the field has converged on.

From Vision Transformers to video

The Vision Transformer (ViT) established the pattern that video models inherit: cut the input into fixed-size patches, linearly project each patch into a vector (a token), add a positional embedding, and hand the resulting sequence to a standard Transformer encoder. For a single image, a patch is a square crop of pixels: typically 16×16.

Extending this approach to video also requires dividing the time dimension. A common solution is the tubelet embedding. Instead of using a flat 16×16 patch, the model takes a small 3D block, such as 2 frames of 16×16 pixels, and projects it into one token. Each tubelet token therefore contains a small amount of local motion information before attention begins.

Worked example

A 224×224, 32-frame clip with 16×16×2 tubelets produces (224/16) × (224/16) × (32/2) = 14 × 14 × 16 = 3,136 tokens. Compare that to treating every pixel as a token: over 1.6 million. Tubelet embedding is the first and largest compression step in the whole pipeline.

Spatiotemporal attention: the cost problem

Self-attention works by having every token look at every other token and decide how much to "pay attention" to it: that pairwise comparison is what lets the model connect a moving object in frame 1 to the same object in frame 30. Because every token must compare itself against every other token, the number of comparisons is not proportional to the number of tokens (n): it's proportional to the number of pairs you can form from n tokens, which is n × n = n². With 4 tokens there are 4×4 = 16 pairs; with 10 tokens there are 10×10 = 100 pairs; with 3,136 tokens there are 3,136×3,136 ≈ 9.8 million pairs. This n-squared relationship is exactly what "O(n²)" (quadratic) means: double the tokens, and the cost roughly quadruples, not doubles: which is why the four bars below get so dramatically longer even though the token counts only grow in modest steps.

Three factorizations are used in practice, each trading some modeling power for tractability:

Divided space-time attention

Factor joint spatiotemporal attention into two stages: spatial attention among the S tokens in each frame, then temporal attention across the T frames for each spatial position. TimeSformer and many video ViTs use variants of this design. With n = ST total tokens, attention cost falls from O((ST)²) to O(TS² + ST²).

The saving comes with a structural bias. Under joint attention, any two spatiotemporal tokens can exchange information through one attention edge. Under divided attention, tokens that differ in both time and position communicate indirectly through the spatial and temporal stages. This factorization can make correspondences involving fast motion, large displacement, occlusion, or camera movement harder to learn, although it does not necessarily require multiple blocks: sequential sublayers can already provide an indirect path within one block. Deeper stacks, motion-aware features, or occasional global-attention layers can help, but divided attention is still not equivalent to joint attention. The formula above covers only the attention-score computation; actual speedups also depend on projections, MLPs, memory traffic, and implementation.

Windowed / local attention

Restrict each token to a local spatiotemporal neighborhood, such as a 3D window of nearby tubelets. If each window contains W tokens, the attention cost is roughly O(nW) instead of O(n²). Some architectures shift the windows between layers so that information can eventually cross the original boundaries. This extends the windowing idea used by Swin Transformer to video.

The tradeoff is weaker access to distant context. Tokens in separate windows cannot interact in the same attention operation, so long distance relationships must pass through shifted windows or later layers. Small windows save more computation but may split one object or motion track across boundaries. Larger windows preserve more context but cost more memory and computation. Shifted windows, overlapping windows, and occasional global layers reduce this problem, but they do not remove the basic locality constraint.

Hierarchical / pooled attention

Reduce the token grid as the network gets deeper, much like the gradual reduction of feature map resolution in a CNN. Early layers process fine detail at high resolution. Later layers use fewer tokens, so they can cover a larger spatial and temporal range at lower cost. The exact saving depends on when downsampling occurs and by how much.

The tradeoff is loss of resolution. Once several tokens have been merged, later layers may no longer know exactly where or when a small event occurred. Aggressive pooling can therefore hurt small object recognition, precise spatial or temporal localization, and brief actions. It can also introduce aliasing if the downsampling step does not preserve the relevant signal. Multiscale features, skip connections, and gentler pooling schedules can retain more detail, but discarded information cannot be recovered perfectly.

The practical takeaway: when you read a video model's architecture section and see terms like divided attention, window size, or pooling stride, they are all answers to the same question: how much of the O(n²) cost can be avoided without losing the long-range dependencies that make video understanding possible.

Video tokenization: turning pixels into model-ready codes

Tubelet embedding produces continuous vectors: it compresses, but it does not give you a discrete vocabulary the way word tokenization does for text. Many modern video generation and world models add a second step: a learned video tokenizer, usually a VQ-VAE-style autoencoder, that maps a video clip into a grid of discrete codes from a fixed codebook, then reconstructs the video from those codes.

The codebook size is a design choice, not something derived from the clip's dimensions: 16,384 (2¹⁴) is a common pick because VQGAN and several follow-up video tokenizers popularized it: a power-of-two codebook in the 8,192–16,384 range tends to give the autoencoder enough distinct codes to reconstruct fine detail, without the embedding table and code-prediction head becoming unnecessarily large.

The choice between discrete codes and continuous latents determines which generative objective can be used above the tokenizer. With discrete codes, a video becomes a sequence of integer codebook indices. The model can then learn to predict the next index from the preceding indices, much like next-token prediction in a language model. With continuous latents, there is no fixed vocabulary of indices. A diffusion model can instead learn to turn noise gradually into a video latent, while flow matching learns a continuous path from noise to data. Both approaches are widely used, and neither is universally best.

In either case, the tokenizer is a compression bottleneck. It must preserve enough information in the compressed codes or latents to reconstruct the source video, but some detail will inevitably be lost. Downstream generation, prediction, and understanding cannot recover information that the tokenizer has already discarded. For example, if small text becomes unreadable after tokenization, later model stages cannot reconstruct the original characters reliably. Tokenizer quality therefore places an upper bound on the entire system.

Latent diffusion: generating in a compressed space

Diffusion models generate data by learning to reverse a gradual noising process: start from pure noise and iteratively denoise toward a realistic sample. Doing this directly on pixels is extremely expensive for video: each denoising step touches every pixel of every frame. Latent diffusion solves this by running the entire diffusion process inside the compressed latent space produced by a video autoencoder, then decoding only the final result back to pixels.

The idea is related to latent diffusion for images, but video autoencoders must also compress and reconstruct time. A video encoder maps a clip to a smaller latent tensor; a diffusion or flow-based model generates in that space under text or other conditions; and a decoder reconstructs the full-resolution video. Compression factors are architecture-specific rather than universal constants.

Pixel-space diffusion

  • Denoises every pixel, every step
  • Highest fidelity ceiling, highest compute cost
  • Rarely used for video at scale today

Latent diffusion

  • Denoises a compressed latent tensor
  • Fidelity capped by the autoencoder's reconstruction quality
  • What Cosmos, Sora-style models, and most production video generators use

Temporal consistency: the problem unique to video

An image generator only has to produce one internally consistent frame. A video generator has to produce hundreds of frames that are each internally consistent and consistent with every other frame: the same object should keep its identity, shape, and texture as it moves, and lighting should not flicker arbitrarily between frames. This failure mode, where a generated video looks locally plausible frame-by-frame but incoherent over time, is called temporal inconsistency or flicker.

Three mechanisms are used, often together, to enforce consistency:

  • Temporal attention layers: attention across the time axis (see above) lets every frame's generation condition on neighboring frames directly, rather than generating each frame independently.
  • 3D convolutions / causal temporal layers in the autoencoder, so the latent representation itself already encodes short-range motion continuity before generation even starts.
  • Autoregressive / rolling generation: generating a video in overlapping chunks where each new chunk is conditioned on the end of the previous one, trading some long-range coherence for the ability to generate arbitrarily long videos.
Why this matters for Cosmos Temporal reasoning requires the model's visual tokenization, positional encoding, and attention stack to preserve and relate evidence across frames; temporal attention is one possible mechanism, not a guarantee of correct timestamps. For generated data, temporal consistency is likewise necessary but not sufficient: flicker and identity swaps are obvious defects, while subtler physical errors require separate evaluation.
