Phase 1 · Topic 03

Multimodal / Physical AI

Connecting vision to language, video understanding to video generation, and passive observation to action-conditioned prediction: the grounding layer between a world model and the physical world it describes.

What "multimodal" means here

A video representation captures what a camera sees. A world model reasons about how that scene evolves. Neither, by itself, connects to human-readable language or to the world beyond the camera's field of view. Multimodal models close that gap by learning a shared representation space across modalities: most commonly vision and language. This ensures that an image or video and a text description of it end up close together in the same embedding space.

"Physical AI" is NVIDIA's term for the further step: models that don't just describe the physical world in language, but connect perception to physically grounded action or simulation: robots, autonomous systems, and camera-driven infrastructure that has to reason about real, embodied consequences, not just generate plausible-looking pictures.

Vision-language models: how the connection is learned

The dominant recipe (established by CLIP and its successors) trains two encoders: one for images, one for text: with a contrastive objective: for a batch of (image, caption) pairs, push each image's embedding close to its matching caption's embedding, and push it away from every other caption in the batch. No manual labels beyond naturally occurring image-caption pairs are required.

Extending this to video mostly means extending the vision encoder to a video encoder (see the previous topic's tubelet embedding and spatiotemporal attention) while keeping the same contrastive idea, now aligning video clips with captions or narrations. The result is a model that can, for instance, retrieve the video clip that best matches a text query, or score how well a generated caption matches a given clip: both of which turn out to be reusable as evaluation tools, not just generation components.

Understanding and generation: two directions, one representation

Video understanding

  • Video + question → text answer or structured output
  • Cosmos Reasoner examples: temporal grounding and event explanation
  • Errors show up as wrong or hallucinated answers

Video generation

  • Text or condition → new video pixels
  • Cosmos Generator examples: synthetic scenario variants
  • Errors show up as implausible or physically wrong footage

These capabilities may share training data, positional representations, or parts of a transformer stack, but they do not necessarily use the same tokenizer or output head. Their objectives also differ: understanding commonly uses causal next-token prediction for text, whereas generation may use diffusion or flow objectives over visual, audio, or action latents. Cosmos 3 unifies the two modes within a Mixture-of-Transformers architecture while retaining different token-processing paths.

Action-conditioned models

An action-conditioned model predicts video (or state) not just from past frames, but from past frames plus a specific action: given this scene and this steering input, what happens next? This is the mechanism that turns a passive world model into something usable for planning: it is literally the transition function from the previous topic, made concrete with real action vectors (steering angle, throttle, robot joint torques, etc.).

Training this requires paired (observation, action, next observation) data: which is exactly what's expensive and hard to collect at scale for real robots or vehicles, and exactly why synthetic data generation is so attractive if it can be trusted: a simulator or a generative world model can produce unlimited (state, action, next-state) triples without needing a physical robot to execute every one of them.

Synthetic data: promise and the trap

Synthetic data can be cheaper than collecting equivalent real-world cases and can deliberately target rare conditions such as night, fog, or wrong-way traffic. A simulator with explicit scene state may also export precise labels. A generative video model, however, does not automatically provide perfect ground truth: the realized pixels can diverge from the prompt or conditioning signal, and any automatically derived labels still require validation.

The trap is the same one raised under counterfactual simulation: a generator trained to produce visually convincing video is optimized for looking right, not for being physically or statistically representative of the real distribution it's meant to stand in for. A model trained partly or wholly on synthetic data can end up learning the generator's biases and blind spots rather than the real world's actual statistics: a failure mode sometimes called domain gap or, in the worst case, model collapse from training on your own outputs.

The decision rule this topic justifies Treat synthetic data as an experiment whose value must be proven against held-out real footage, never as ground truth by default. This is not caution for its own sake: it follows directly from the fact that generation quality and real-world representativeness are two different properties that a model can have independently.
